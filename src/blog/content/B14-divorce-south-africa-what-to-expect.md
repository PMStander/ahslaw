# Divorce in South Africa: What to Expect and How to Protect Yourself

Deciding to end a marriage is one of the hardest decisions most people ever make. The emotional weight is heavy enough — the legal process can feel like navigating a maze of unfamiliar terms, forms, and court dates. That uncertainty often makes everything harder.

This guide explains, in plain language, how divorce actually works in South Africa. It covers the grounds, what happens to your assets, how the children are protected, and the practical steps involved. It won't take away the emotion, but it will help you understand the road ahead and the decisions that matter most.

## The Only Real Ground: Irretrievable Breakdown

Under the Divorce Act 70 of 1979, a court will grant a divorce when the marriage has broken down irretrievably — meaning there is no reasonable prospect of restoring a normal marriage relationship. South Africa is a no-fault jurisdiction. You do not need to prove that anyone was "at fault."

Adultery, abuse, or simply growing apart can all serve as evidence of the breakdown, but one spouse wanting the divorce is usually enough in practice.

There are two narrower grounds as well — the mental illness or continuous unconsciousness of a spouse — but these are rare and come with strict requirements.

## What Happens to Your Assets Depends on How You Married

This is the question we are asked most often. The honest answer is: it depends entirely on your matrimonial property regime — the terms on which you married. That decision is made long before any divorce, usually on (or before) your wedding day.

| Regime | What It Means on Divorce |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **In Community of Property** (default if you signed no antenuptial contract) | You share one joint estate. Assets and debts are divided equally — a 50/50 split of everything. |
| **Out of Community with Accrual** | Each spouse keeps their own estate, but the growth (accrual) built up during the marriage is shared. The spouse whose estate grew less can claim half the difference. |
| **Out of Community without Accrual** | "What's mine is mine, what's yours is yours." Each spouse keeps their own estate entirely, with no automatic sharing of growth. |

If you are unsure which applies to you, dig out your antenuptial contract. If you never signed one, you are almost certainly married in community of property. For a fuller look at how these regimes work before and during a marriage, see our guide to [community of property vs accrual](/blog/community-of-property-vs-accrual).

## An Important Recent Change for "Out of Community Without Accrual"

For years, a spouse married out of community without accrual could walk away from a long marriage with very little — even after decades of raising children and running a home while the other built up the wealth. That has changed.

In October 2023, in the landmark Constitutional Court judgment *EB (born S) v ER (born B) and Others; KG v Minister of Home Affairs and Others* [2023] ZACC 32, the Court declared the old limits on redistribution unconstitutional. The effect is that a court can now, in appropriate cases, order a redistribution of assets in these marriages where one spouse made significant contributions — financial or non-financial — to the growth of the other's estate.

Parliament is still in the process of writing these protections fully into the legislation, but the judgment already applies. If you are married out of community without accrual and worried about being left with nothing, this development may be very important for you. Early legal advice can make a real difference to the outcome.

## Uncontested vs Contested Divorce

An **uncontested divorce** is where both spouses agree on everything — the division of assets, maintenance, and the care of any children. These terms are recorded in a settlement agreement, which the court makes an order. Uncontested divorces are faster, far cheaper, and much less stressful. They can sometimes be finalised in a matter of weeks.

A **contested divorce** is where the spouses cannot agree on one or more issues. The matter proceeds through the courts and can take many months (occasionally longer). Most contested divorces still settle before trial once the issues narrow and emotions cool — but they require careful legal handling to protect your position along the way.

Wherever possible, we steer clients towards settlement. It is almost always in everyone's interests, and especially the children's.

## The Children Come First

Where there are minor children, the court's guiding principle is the best interests of the child — nothing else. The parents' wishes are secondary to what is best for the child.

You will need to agree on, or ask the court to decide:

- **Care and contact** — where the children live and how they spend time with each parent (the old terms were "custody" and "access").
- **Maintenance** — the financial support each parent contributes towards the children's needs.

The Office of the Family Advocate may become involved to assess what arrangement serves the children best. A well-drafted parenting plan can prevent years of future conflict. Do not treat this as a box-ticking exercise — the arrangements you put in place now will shape your children's lives for years.

## What About Spousal Maintenance?

Spousal maintenance — one spouse supporting the other after divorce — is not automatic. A court considers factors such as the length of the marriage, each spouse's earning capacity, age, health, and the standard of living during the marriage. It may order maintenance for a fixed period, until death or remarriage, or not at all. Every case turns on its own facts.

## The Process, Step by Step

While every divorce is different, the path usually looks like this:

1. **Consultation and preparation** — We assess your marital regime, assets, and the issues in dispute.
2. **Summons** — Divorce proceedings are started by issuing a summons in the Regional Court or the High Court.
3. **Negotiation** — The vast majority of the work is often in reaching a fair settlement agreement.
4. **Court date** — For an uncontested divorce, this is usually a brief, straightforward appearance.
5. **Decree of divorce** — Once granted, this is the order that legally ends the marriage and gives effect to the agreed terms.

## Common Myths

- **"If I leave the house, I lose my rights."** False. Moving out to protect yourself or the children does not forfeit your claim to the property or your role as a parent.
- **"Divorce always takes years."** Not true. An uncontested divorce can be relatively quick. It is the disputes — not the divorce itself — that cause delay.
- **"We were never legally married on paper, so I have no claim."** Be careful here. Depending on the circumstances, you may still have rights. Get advice before assuming you have none.
- **"Whoever files first has the advantage."** No. Who issues the summons first has no bearing on the outcome. The court decides on the merits.
- **"I can stop contact if maintenance isn't paid."** No. Contact and maintenance are separate. Withholding one to force the other is not permitted and can count against you.
