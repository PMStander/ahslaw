# Maintenance Unpaid? Exactly What to Do Next

School fees are due on Friday. The fridge is nearly empty. Your ex was supposed to pay maintenance two weeks ago and the money never came. The phone goes to voicemail. The messages stay on "delivered."

If that is your week, take a breath. The law is firmly on your side, and there is a clear, free process to fix this. This guide walks you through it, step by step, in plain English.

## First, a quick reminder: this is not a "fathers only" issue

Under the **Children's Act 38 of 2005** and the **Maintenance Act 99 of 1998**, **both parents are legally obligated to support their children**. Whether the parents were married — and if so, [which marital regime they were under](/blog/community-of-property-vs-accrual) — can also affect how assets and arrears are pursued. It does not matter who the children live with, whether the parents were ever married, or who earns more on paper. Each parent contributes in proportion to their means.

So whether you are a mother chasing the father, a father chasing the mother, or a guardian chasing either parent, the process is the same. The court does not pick sides based on gender — it looks at the child's needs and each parent's ability to pay.

## What maintenance must actually cover

Maintenance is not just "pocket money." It must cover the reasonable cost of raising the child, including:

- **Food** — daily meals and lunchbox
- **Housing** — a fair share of rent, water, electricity
- **Education** — school fees, uniforms, stationery, transport
- **Medical and dental** — medical aid contributions, doctor visits, medication, dentist
- **Recreation** — sport, extramurals, age-appropriate activities
- **Clothing** — including replacements as the child grows

The figure should reflect the standard of living the child is reasonably entitled to, not the bare minimum to survive.

## Step 1: Document everything

Before you go anywhere, sit down for thirty minutes and build a paper trail. The Maintenance Officer will ask for it, and a clean record makes everything faster.

Write down or print out:

- The **date** maintenance was due each month and the **date** (if any) it was paid
- The **amount** that should have been paid versus what was actually paid
- All **bank statements** showing the missing or short payments
- Screenshots of **WhatsApp messages, emails, or texts** where you asked about the payment
- Any **court order, settlement agreement, or written arrangement** you already have
- Receipts and invoices showing what the child needs (school fees, medical bills, groceries)

Keep originals safe. Make two copies of everything.

## Step 2: Visit your local Magistrate's Court Maintenance Office

Every Magistrate's Court in South Africa has a **Maintenance Office**. You go to the one closest to where **you and the child live**, not where your ex lives.

You do **not** need an attorney. You do **not** need money. The maintenance process is free.

Walk in during business hours and ask to speak to the Maintenance Officer or the Maintenance Clerk. Tell them you want to lodge a maintenance complaint (if there is no order yet) or report a breach of an existing maintenance order.

## Step 3: File a complaint — what to bring

Pack a simple folder with:

- Your **South African ID** (or passport)
- The **child's birth certificate**
- Your **payslip or proof of income** (or proof you are unemployed)
- A **list of monthly expenses** for the child (rent share, school fees, food, medical, transport)
- The **existing court order**, if you have one
- Your **bank statements** showing the missed payments
- Any **contact details** you have for the other parent — full name, ID number if known, employer, address, phone numbers

The clerk will help you complete the relevant form (typically a Form A, J101 or J103, depending on whether it is a new application or an enforcement complaint). Take your time. Ask if you do not understand a section.

## Step 4: The Maintenance Officer's investigation

Once your complaint is registered, a **Maintenance Officer** is assigned to your file. Their job is to investigate before the matter goes before a magistrate.

The officer will:

- **Summons** the other parent to come in and provide proof of income, expenses, and assets
- **Interview** both parties separately or together
- Where possible, try to **negotiate a consent order** so you do not need a contested hearing
- Refer the matter to a **Maintenance Court hearing** if the parent refuses, fails to attend, or disputes everything

This is also the stage where the officer can ask employers, banks, and SARS for information about the other parent's true income.

## Step 5: The Maintenance Court hearing — what to expect

If a hearing is needed, it happens at the same Magistrate's Court. It is not a criminal trial. The atmosphere is more like a structured conversation in front of a magistrate.

You will be asked about:

- The child's reasonable monthly needs
- Your income and what you already contribute
- The other parent's income and lifestyle
- Any existing arrangement and why it has broken down

The magistrate then issues a **maintenance order** — a legally binding document specifying who pays, how much, when, and how (usually directly into your bank account or via the court).

## Enforcement when an order is breached

This is the part most people do not realise. Once there is an order in place and the other parent stops paying, the law gives you serious teeth:

- **Garnishee order on salary (emoluments attachment order)** — the court orders the employer to deduct maintenance directly from the parent's salary before they ever see it.
- **Warrant of arrest** — failure to pay maintenance is a **criminal offence** under section 31 of the Maintenance Act. The magistrate can issue a warrant. A conviction carries a fine and/or up to **one year's imprisonment**. This is one of the few areas where non-payment genuinely can lead to arrest — unlike ordinary civil debt, [which cannot result in arrest in South Africa](/blog/you-cannot-be-arrested-for-debt).
- **Attachment of property** — the sheriff can attach and sell movable property (car, electronics, furniture) to satisfy the arrears.
- **Civil execution** — arrears can be recovered as a civil debt, including from bank accounts and other assets.
- **Credit bureau listing** — defaulters can be reported to credit bureaus, which damages their credit record until they pay.

You do not have to choose just one. The Maintenance Officer can pursue several at once.

## What if the other parent has disappeared?

You can still apply. The court has the power to **trace** a defaulting parent through:

- **SAPS** assistance to locate them
- **Department of Home Affairs** records
- **SARS, banks, and employers** via subpoena
- Tracing through their **last known address** and family contacts

Bring whatever you have — old phone numbers, social media handles, a recent employer name — and the court will work with it.

## What if your circumstances change — yours or theirs?

Life happens. If the paying parent loses their job, gets retrenched, or genuinely cannot pay, they must apply to the court to **vary** (reduce or pause) the order. They cannot simply stop paying.

The same applies to you. If your costs rise — the child starts a new school, has a medical issue, or your own income drops — you can apply to **increase** maintenance. Bring the new figures and proof.

A court order is not a life sentence. It is a snapshot that can be updated when reality changes. What you cannot do is ignore it.

## You are not alone in this

Single parents are exhausted. Chasing money your child is legally entitled to should not be your second job. The maintenance system is not perfect, but it works — especially when you go in prepared with documents, dates and a calm plan.

If the process feels overwhelming, or if the other parent has lawyers, hidden income, or is using delay tactics, an attorney can carry the file for you.
