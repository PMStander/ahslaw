# POPIA: Can Your Employer Read Your WhatsApp?

Your phone is in your hand for roughly twelve hours a day. You scroll it on the couch, on the toilet, in meetings, in bed. Some of those messages are about groceries. Some are about your health. Some are conversations you would never want your boss to read.

So here is the question almost every South African worker has asked at least once: **what is your employer actually allowed to see?**

The short answer is: less than most bosses think, and more than most employees realise. The law that draws the line is POPIA — the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 — and in 2026 the Information Regulator is finally putting real teeth behind it.

Here is the plain-English version.

## POPIA in 60 Seconds

POPIA stands for the **Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013**. It came into full force in July 2021. In one sentence: it gives every South African legal control over the personal information that companies, government departments, and yes, employers, hold about you.

POPIA applies to anyone who processes personal information in South Africa — that includes your employer, your bank, your medical aid, your gym, the corner shop with a loyalty programme, and the marketing company that keeps SMSing you about funeral cover.

"Personal information" is broader than people expect. It includes your name, ID number, address, contact details, biometric data, financial data, opinions you have expressed, your race, religion, beliefs, your medical history, and any unique identifier such as an employee number or device ID.

The rules are enforced by an independent body called the **Information Regulator**.

## Your Six Core Rights Under POPIA

Whether you are an employee, a customer, or a citizen, POPIA gives you six rights you can exercise at any time:

1. **The right to know** what personal information is held about you, by whom, and why.
2. **The right to access** that information — you can ask for a copy.
3. **The right to correction** of inaccurate or outdated information.
4. **The right to deletion** of information that is no longer necessary, lawful, or accurate.
5. **The right to object** to processing that causes you unjustified harm or that is used for direct marketing.
6. **The right to complain** to the Information Regulator — for free.

These rights apply to your employer the same way they apply to a bank. Knowing this changes the conversation.

## Workplace Surveillance: What Your Employer Can and Cannot Do

This is where the line gets practical.

### Work email and work devices: yes, with notice

If your employer issues you a laptop, a work phone, or a company email address, they are allowed to monitor what happens on those systems. **But only if they have told you first.**

A clear, written workplace privacy or acceptable-use policy is the foundation. If you signed a contract or policy that says "company devices and email are monitored for security and compliance purposes," your employer is on solid legal ground when reviewing those communications. Covert monitoring without notice is a different story — it requires specific legal justification and is hard to defend.

The takeaway: assume everything on your work laptop and work email is visible to your employer. Treat it that way.

### Personal WhatsApp on your personal phone: no

This one is the easy answer. If WhatsApp lives on your own phone, on your own number, and you are not on a company network at the time, your employer has **no legal right** to read those messages.

Asking to see them, demanding screenshots, or threatening discipline if you refuse to hand over your phone is a clear POPIA breach — and almost certainly a breach of your constitutional right to privacy under section 14 of the Constitution as well.

### WhatsApp on a work phone? The grey area

This is where it gets murky, and where most disputes happen.

If WhatsApp is installed on a phone your employer owns and pays the contract for, the employer's position is stronger — but it is not unlimited. The test the courts apply (and the Information Regulator broadly follows) is **reasonable expectation of privacy.**

Two questions decide it:

- Did the employer tell you, in writing, that WhatsApp on this device may be monitored?
- Is the monitoring proportionate to a legitimate business purpose (security, fraud prevention, regulatory compliance) — or is it a fishing expedition?

If the answer to the first is no, or to the second is no, the employer is on thin ice no matter who owns the SIM card. Werksmans and other leading firms have been clear in 2026 commentary: "we own the device" is not a free pass. Notice and proportionality are non-negotiable.

### CCTV in common work areas: yes, with notice

Cameras in receptions, corridors, warehouses, parking lots and open-plan offices are generally lawful — provided employees and visitors are told they are being recorded. A visible sign at the entrance and a line in the staff handbook does the job.

### CCTV in toilets, change rooms, and private spaces: never

There is no version of South African law where this is acceptable. Cameras in toilets, change rooms, showers, lactation rooms, prayer rooms, or any space where people undress or attend to bodily functions are unlawful. Full stop. If you discover one, that is a Labour Court matter, a POPIA complaint, and potentially a criminal matter all at once.

## Direct Marketing: The Spam Rules

POPIA also covers the avalanche of unsolicited messages most South Africans receive every week.

For **electronic direct marketing** — email, SMS, WhatsApp messages, automated calls — the rule is **opt-in consent.** A company has to get your active permission before sending you marketing. There is one narrow exception: existing customers can be marketed to about similar products, but only until they opt out.

Every marketing message must include:

- The identity of the sender
- A clear, free way to opt out

If a company keeps messaging you after you have opted out, or never had your consent in the first place, that is a POPIA breach you can report.

## The 2026 Information Regulator Enforcement Push

For the first few years after POPIA came into force, enforcement was light. That changed in 2026.

In March 2026 the Information Regulator announced sharper enforcement priorities, launched a **centralised eServices portal** for complaints and compliance reporting, and brought new health data regulations into force. The first major fines are expected this year, and businesses across South Africa are scrambling to do the personal information impact assessments they should have done years ago.

The practical message for employees: complaints are easier to file than ever. The practical message for employers: "we'll get to it later" is no longer a defensible position. The [2026 Labour Law Amendment Bill](/blog/labour-law-amendment-bill-2026) reinforces this further — it now holds employers directly liable for discriminatory outcomes from AI tools used in recruitment and HR, which the Information Regulator and the Commission for Employment Equity both have standing to investigate.

## The R10 Million Penalty

POPIA is not a polite suggestion. The maximum administrative fine for a serious breach is **R10 million**, and the Act also allows for **up to 10 years imprisonment** for the worst offences (such as obstructing the Regulator or deliberate breaches involving sensitive personal information). Civil damages claims by affected data subjects are available on top of that.

For employers, that is a real number. For employees, it is leverage.

## How to File a POPIA Complaint

If you believe your privacy has been breached — by your employer, a marketing company, or anyone else — the process is straightforward and free:

1. **Document the breach.** Save messages, screenshots, dates, and any policy or contract that is relevant.
2. **Raise it internally first** if you can. Many companies will resolve complaints once their information officer is alerted.
3. **File with the Information Regulator** via the eServices portal at **inforegulator.org.za**. The complaint form (POPIA Form 5) walks you through it.
4. **Keep a copy** of everything you submit and any response you receive.

The Regulator can investigate, issue enforcement notices, and impose administrative fines. You do not need a lawyer to file — but if the breach is serious, or your employer has retaliated, getting legal advice early is worth it.
