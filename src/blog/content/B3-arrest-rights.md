# Your Rights When Arrested in South Africa — A 5-Minute Guide

Most people freeze when they hear the words "you are under arrest." That is normal. The next five minutes will give you everything you need to stay calm, stay safe, and protect yourself.

South Africa has one of the most rights-protective constitutions in the world when it comes to arrest. The problem is that very few people know what those rights actually are. This guide walks you through Section 35 of the Constitution in plain English, so you can act with confidence if it ever happens to you or someone you love.

## Section 35 — Your Rights at a Glance

Section 35 of the Constitution sets out exactly what every arrested person is entitled to. Memorise the table below.

| Your Right | What It Means in Practice |
|---|---|
| **Right to remain silent** | You do not have to answer questions about the alleged offence. |
| **Right to be informed** | The police must tell you, in a language you understand, why you are being arrested. |
| **Right to legal representation** | You are entitled to speak to a lawyer before answering any questions. |
| **Right to legal aid** | If you cannot afford a lawyer, the state must provide one through Legal Aid South Africa. |
| **Right to court within 48 hours** | You must be brought before a court within 48 hours of arrest, or by the next court day if those hours fall on a weekend or public holiday. |
| **Right to humane treatment** | You may not be tortured, assaulted, or treated in a cruel or degrading way. |
| **Right to communicate** | You may notify a family member, friend, or attorney of your arrest and where you are being held. |

These rights apply from the moment of arrest. Not at the police station. Not after questioning. The moment.

## The Right to Remain Silent — What to Actually Say

This is the single most important thing in this article.

If you are arrested, calmly say these words: **"I am exercising my right to remain silent. I would like to speak to a lawyer."**

Then stop talking. Do not explain. Do not justify. Do not "clear things up." You are only legally required to give your name and address. Everything else is optional, and anything you say can be used against you in court.

Police officers may try to get you to talk by being friendly, by suggesting that silence will make things worse, or by saying it will be quicker if you just answer. None of that changes your right. Stay polite, repeat the sentence above if needed, and wait for your lawyer.

## The Right to Legal Representation

You have the right to a lawyer **before** you are questioned. Not afterwards. If you have a family attorney, ask to call them. If you do not, ask for a Legal Aid attorney.

Legal Aid South Africa is a national service that provides free legal representation to people who cannot afford private lawyers in serious criminal matters. The duty to inform you of this falls on the police. If they have not mentioned it, ask for it directly.

Until your lawyer arrives, do not sign anything and do not participate in identification parades, statements, or pointing-out exercises. Politely decline and wait.

## The 48-Hour Rule

Police cannot hold you indefinitely. Within 48 hours of your arrest, they must bring you before a court so that the court can either formally charge you, grant bail, or order further detention with reasons.

If those 48 hours expire on a weekend or public holiday, the deadline extends to the end of the first court day after that. So an arrest on a Friday evening generally means a first appearance on the following Monday.

If you are not brought to court within this period, your detention becomes unlawful and your attorney can apply for your immediate release.

## Arrest With and Without a Warrant

Police may arrest you in two ways.

**With a warrant:** A magistrate has issued a written authorisation, naming you and the offence. You are entitled to ask to see the warrant.

**Without a warrant:** Section 40 of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977 allows warrantless arrest only in defined circumstances — most commonly where the officer personally witnesses an offence, or has a reasonable suspicion that you have committed a Schedule 1 offence (serious crimes such as murder, rape, robbery, housebreaking, or fraud over R100,000).

A reasonable suspicion is a real legal test. It is not a hunch. The officer must be able to articulate facts that would lead a reasonable person to the same conclusion.

## Search and Seizure — What Police Can and Cannot Do

If you are lawfully arrested, police may search your person and your immediate surroundings — for example, the seat of the car you were driving — without a separate warrant.

To search your home or business premises, they generally need a search warrant issued by a magistrate. There are narrow exceptions: hot pursuit, consent that you freely give, or circumstances where evidence would obviously be destroyed before a warrant could be obtained.

A warrant for your **arrest** does not automatically authorise a search of your **home**. These are two different documents.

If officers ask permission to search, you may refuse. State politely: "I do not consent to a search." That refusal cannot be used against you.

## What to Do If Your Rights Were Violated

If you believe your rights were violated — you were assaulted, denied a lawyer, held beyond 48 hours, or searched without authority — take these steps:

1. **Note everything.** Names, badge numbers, vehicle numbers, times, witnesses.
2. **Get medical attention** immediately if you were injured, and ask for a J88 medico-legal form to be completed.
3. **Tell your lawyer.** Unlawfully obtained evidence can be excluded under Section 35(5) of the Constitution.
4. **Lay a complaint** with IPID (the Independent Police Investigative Directorate), which investigates police misconduct.
5. **Consider a civil claim** for unlawful arrest or detention. There is a strict time limit — speak to an attorney quickly.

## Three Common Misconceptions

**"I have to answer police questions."** No. You must give your name and address. Everything else is your choice.

**"I can be arrested for an unpaid debt."** No. Civil debt is not a criminal matter. [You cannot be arrested for owing money](/blog/you-cannot-be-arrested-for-debt) — not a credit card, not a clothing account, not a car payment. Debt collectors who say otherwise are lying.

**"A warrant for my arrest lets them search my house."** No. Arrest warrants and search warrants are separate. They need both.

**"I can resist an unlawful arrest."** Do not. Even if the arrest is unlawful, physical resistance can lead to additional charges and serious injury. Comply, stay silent, and challenge the arrest in court through your lawyer.
