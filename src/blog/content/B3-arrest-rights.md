# Your Rights When Arrested in South Africa — A Practical Guide

Most people go blank when they hear "You're under arrest." That's completely normal. In those first stressful moments, knowing your basic rights can help you stay calm, protect yourself, and avoid making things worse.

South Africa's Constitution offers strong protections for people who are arrested. This short guide explains them in plain language, with key points from the **Criminal Procedure Act (CPA)** that give those rights real power on the ground.

## Your Core Rights Under Section 35 of the Constitution

Section 35 spells out what every arrested person is entitled to, right from the moment the police detain you:

| Your Right | What It Means in Practice |
|---|---|
| **Right to remain silent** | You don't have to answer questions about the alleged offence. Police must inform you of this right and the consequences of not remaining silent. |
| **Right to be informed** | Officers must promptly tell you, in a language you understand, why you are being arrested and what the charge is. |
| **Right not to be compelled to confess** | No one can force you to make a statement or give evidence that could be used against you. |
| **Right to legal representation** | You can consult a lawyer of your choice before answering any questions. |
| **Right to legal aid** | If you can't afford a private lawyer, you're entitled to one through Legal Aid South Africa. |
| **Right to appear in court quickly** | You must be brought before a court within 48 hours of arrest (or on the next court day if it falls over a weekend or public holiday). |
| **Right to humane treatment** | You may not be tortured, assaulted, or treated in a cruel, inhuman, or degrading way. |
| **Right to communicate** | You can notify a family member, friend, or your lawyer of your arrest and where you are being held. |

These rights kick in **immediately** — not only once you reach the police station.

## The Right to Remain Silent — Your Strongest Shield

This is the most important advice in this guide.

If arrested, calmly say:

**"I am exercising my right to remain silent. I want to speak to a lawyer."**

Then stop talking. You only need to give your name and address if asked. Anything else is optional, and police can (and will) use it against you later.

Officers might act friendly, pressure you to "just clear it up," or suggest that silence looks suspicious. Stay polite but firm. Repeat your statement if needed and wait for your lawyer.

## Your Right to a Lawyer

You are entitled to speak to a lawyer **before** any questioning or making a statement. Ask for your own attorney if you have one, or contact one of ours.

**Until your lawyer arrives, do not:**
- Sign any documents
- Make a statement
- Participate in an identification parade
- Do any pointing-out exercises

Section 35(1) of the Constitution and relevant CPA provisions back this up strongly.

## The 48-Hour Rule (Section 50 of the CPA)

Police cannot hold you forever. You must be brought before a court within 48 hours (or the next court day). The court then decides on charges, bail, or further detention.

If this deadline is missed without good reason, your continued detention becomes unlawful. Your lawyer can apply for immediate release.

## Arrest With or Without a Warrant

**With a warrant:** A magistrate or judge has signed it. You can ask to see it.

**Without a warrant (Section 40 of the CPA):** Police may arrest you only in specific situations, for example:
- They personally see you committing an offence, or
- They have reasonable suspicion that you committed a **Schedule 1 offence** (serious crimes like murder, rape, robbery, serious assault, housebreaking, or fraud above certain amounts).

The officer must be able to justify that "reasonable suspicion" later in court.

## Searches and Seizure

- After a lawful arrest, police may search **you** and your immediate surroundings (Section 23 of the CPA).
- To search your home, vehicle, or business, they normally need a separate **search warrant** from a magistrate.
- An arrest warrant does **not** automatically allow a house search.

If officers ask for permission to search, you can politely refuse: **"I do not consent to a search."** Consent can make things easier for them in court.

## What to Do If Your Rights Are Violated

1. Make detailed notes — names, badge numbers, times, what was said, any witnesses.
2. Seek medical attention immediately if injured and request a **J88** medico-legal report.
3. Tell your lawyer — evidence obtained unlawfully can be excluded under Section 35(5) of the Constitution.
4. Report the matter to **IPID** (Independent Police Investigative Directorate).
5. Consider a civil claim for unlawful arrest or detention. Act quickly — there are strict time limits.

## Common Myths

**"I must answer police questions."** False. Only name and address are required. Everything else is your choice.

**"They can arrest me for unpaid debt."** No. [Civil debts are not criminal matters — you cannot be arrested simply for owing money](/blog/you-cannot-be-arrested-for-debt).

**"An arrest warrant lets them search my house."** No. Arrest and search warrants are separate documents.

**"I can resist an unlawful arrest."** Strongly discouraged. Even if the arrest is later found unlawful, physical resistance can lead to extra charges and injury. Comply calmly and challenge it through your lawyer in court.
