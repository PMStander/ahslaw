Most of us spend a large part of our day connected. Work emails, group chats, and personal WhatsApps blur together, especially when people use work phones or check messages during office hours. The practical question that keeps coming up is simple: how much of that can your employer lawfully see, and what happens if they use it against you?

The answer sits at the intersection of four areas of law: the constitutional right to privacy, the Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (POPIA), the Regulation of Interception of Communications and Provision of Communication-Related Information Act 70 of 2002 (RICA), and the Labour Relations Act 66 of 1995 (LRA). None of them gives employers a free hand, and none of them leaves employees without protection.

### The basic legal picture

Section 14 of the Constitution protects the privacy of communications. That right is not absolute in the workplace — the expectation of privacy is lower when you are using company systems — but it does not disappear.

POPIA regulates the processing of personal information. Messages that contain names, contact details, opinions, health information or anything that can identify a person count as personal information. Any access, storage or use of those messages must therefore meet POPIA’s conditions for lawful processing (including purpose limitation, minimality and transparency).

RICA regulates the actual interception of communications. Interception is generally prohibited unless it falls within a recognised exception. The most relevant workplace exception is the “business purpose” provision in section 6: an employer (as system controller) may intercept communications on its own systems if the system is used wholly or partly for the business, the interception is for legitimate business reasons (such as establishing facts, security or authorised use), and the employer has made reasonable efforts to inform users in advance that monitoring may occur — or has obtained consent.

The LRA then governs what an employer can do with any information it obtains. A dismissal based on monitored communications must still be substantively and procedurally fair. Evidence obtained unlawfully can be challenged and may be excluded in CCMA or Labour Court proceedings.

### What employers can and cannot do in practice

**Work email and company systems**
Employers are generally on stronger ground here. If you use a company email address or a company network, and a clear policy or employment contract tells you that these systems may be monitored, the employer can usually review the content for legitimate business reasons. Secret or blanket monitoring without notice remains risky under both RICA and POPIA.

**WhatsApp on a personal phone**
If the app is on your own device, on your own number, and you are not using the company network, the employer has no general right to access those messages. The expectation of privacy is high. Attempting to force access or install monitoring software without proper legal basis will almost always breach RICA and POPIA.

**WhatsApp on a work phone**
This is the grey area that causes the most disputes. Ownership of the device helps the employer, but it is not decisive. Courts and the CCMA look at whether the employee had a reasonable expectation of privacy, whether clear written notice was given, and whether the monitoring was proportionate to a genuine business purpose. “We own the phone” is not enough on its own.

In one well-known CCMA matter (*Smith v Partners in Sexual Health*), an employer accessed an employee’s personal Gmail account that happened to be open on a work computer. The commissioner held that the access violated RICA, the evidence was inadmissible, and the dismissal was unfair. The same reasoning can apply to personal WhatsApp chats on a company device if the employee was not properly notified and the intrusion was not justified.

**Other workplace surveillance**
CCTV in common areas is generally acceptable if people are told about it. Cameras in toilets, change rooms or other private spaces remain unlawful. GPS tracking of company vehicles is usually permitted during working hours if employees know about it. Secret audio recording of conversations by someone who is not a party to the discussion is ordinarily prohibited by RICA.

### Labour law consequences

Even when monitoring is lawful under POPIA and RICA, the employer still has to follow the LRA. The Code of Good Practice: Dismissal requires that employees know the rule, that the rule is reasonable, and that the sanction is consistent and proportional. Evidence obtained through unlawful interception can be ruled inadmissible, which often collapses the employer’s case.

Employees who are disciplined or dismissed on the basis of monitored messages should therefore look carefully at how the information was obtained, whether a proper policy existed, and whether they were given a fair opportunity to respond.

### Practical steps for both sides

Employers who want to stay on the right side of the law should have a clear, written electronic communications and monitoring policy that employees have seen and acknowledged. The policy should explain what is monitored, why, and how the information will be handled. Monitoring should be limited to what is necessary.

Employees who suspect unlawful access should keep records (screenshots, dates, any relevant policies) and, where practical, raise the issue internally first. If that does not resolve it, a complaint can be lodged with the Information Regulator through the eServices portal (eservices.inforegulator.org.za) using POPIA Form 5. Unfair dismissal or unfair labour practice claims can also be referred to the CCMA.

### Penalties and enforcement

Serious breaches of POPIA can attract administrative fines of up to R10 million. Certain RICA offences carry fines or imprisonment of up to 10 years. The Information Regulator has been issuing enforcement notices in 2026, and civil claims for damages remain available.

Workplace monitoring is possible, but it is tightly regulated. Employers who treat it as an unrestricted management right create legal risk for themselves. Employees who assume their messages are completely private on company systems may be surprised. Clear policies, proper notice, and proportionality remain the safest path for both sides.
