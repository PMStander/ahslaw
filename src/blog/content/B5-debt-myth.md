# You Cannot Be Arrested for Debt. Here's What Actually Happens

The phone rings. A man on the other end says he's from "the legal department." He says a warrant of arrest has been issued. He says the police are coming to your home tonight unless you pay R3,400 right now.

Stop. Breathe.

He is lying. And the law is on your side. It also helps to know [exactly what your rights are if you are ever arrested](/blog/your-rights-when-arrested-south-africa) — because those rights apply whether the arrest is lawful or not.

In South Africa, you cannot be arrested for owing money. Not for a clothing account. Not for a personal loan. Not for a car repayment. Not for a credit card. Debt is a civil matter, not a criminal one. No sheriff, no police officer, and no debt collector can put you in handcuffs because you fell behind on a payment.

This blog post explains what the law actually says, what creditors can legally do, and what to do if a debt collector threatens you.

## The Myth That Won't Die

Debt collectors in South Africa make their money by pressure. The faster they scare you, the faster you pay. So they use a script that's been around for decades:

- "We're sending the sheriff to your house."
- "There's a warrant out for your arrest."
- "If you don't pay today, you'll have a criminal record."
- "We'll send the police to your job."

These statements are not mistakes. They are deliberate lies, and using them is illegal under the Debt Collectors Act and the Council for Debt Collectors' Code of Conduct.

The truth is simpler than the threat: failing to pay a civil debt is not a crime. The Constitution does not allow imprisonment for inability to pay. There is no warrant. There is no criminal record. There is no jail cell waiting for you because of an Edgars account.

## What Can Actually Happen if You Don't Pay

A creditor still has real legal options. They are slower, more formal, and more expensive than a phone call. Here is the honest sequence:

1. **Reminders and demand letters.** The creditor contacts you and asks for payment.
2. **A Section 129 notice** under the National Credit Act (NCA).
3. **Summons to appear in court.**
4. **A court judgment** if you do not respond or settle.
5. **Enforcement** through a garnishee order, attachment of goods, or listing on your credit profile.

None of those steps involve arrest. All of them give you opportunities to act, negotiate, or apply for protection.

## Section 129 — Your 10 Business Days

Under Section 129 of the National Credit Act 34 of 2005, before a credit provider can take legal action against you, they must send a written notice to your last known address.

This Section 129 notice gives you **10 business days** to do one of three things:

- Pay the arrears, or
- Contact the credit provider to renegotiate the terms, or
- Apply for debt review

Ignore this notice and you lose powerful protection. The creditor can then issue summons. Read every Section 129 letter the day it arrives. If the address is wrong or the amount is disputed, write back immediately. Keep a copy.

## Garnishee Orders — When They Are Allowed

A garnishee order, properly called an emoluments attachment order (EAO), allows part of your salary to be deducted at source and paid to a creditor. They are real, but they have strict rules.

A garnishee order can only be granted **after a court judgment**. A creditor cannot phone your employer and arrange deductions. They must take you to court, win, and then get an EAO from a magistrate.

Before signing any consent form a debt collector pushes in front of you, ask: "Is there a court judgment against me?" If the answer is no, you do not have to agree.

## The 25% Rule — Your Constitutional Protection

Even with a valid garnishee order, your take-home pay is protected. The combined deductions on your salary cannot push you below a basic survival level. Under current rules, **garnishee deductions cannot exceed 25% of your earnings**.

If you discover that more than a quarter of your salary is being taken — or that deductions have left you unable to afford rent, transport, or food — you have the right to apply to the magistrate's court to **rescind or vary** the order. Bring three months of payslips and a household budget. Magistrates routinely reduce orders that strangle a working person.

Note that **maintenance arrears** are treated differently. Unlike ordinary civil debt, failure to pay a court-ordered maintenance obligation is a criminal offence — an important distinction if your garnishee order relates to maintenance. [Read how maintenance enforcement works and what your options are.](/blog/maintenance-unpaid-what-to-do-next)

If you are married in community of property, your spouse's debts can also become your problem — creditors can pursue the joint estate for money your spouse owes. ## Debt Prescription — Most Debts Expire After 3 Years

Here is a fact creditors do not advertise. Under the Prescription Act, most ordinary debts (credit cards, personal loans, store accounts, cellphone contracts) **prescribe — fall away — after three years** if:

- The creditor has not taken legal action, AND
- You have not made a payment, AND
- You have not acknowledged the debt in writing or by promise.

Once a debt has prescribed, the creditor cannot legally enforce it. They can ask you to pay. They cannot make you.

**The trap:** if you acknowledge the debt — even verbally on a recorded call — the three-year clock resets. So when an old debt suddenly resurfaces, do not say:

- "Yes, I owe that."
- "I'll pay something next month."
- "Send me a settlement offer."

Instead, say: "I do not acknowledge this debt. Please send me proof in writing." Then put the phone down. Some debts that look terrifying on paper are already legally dead.

(Note: home loans and judgment debts have longer prescription periods. If unsure, check with an attorney before assuming any debt has prescribed.)

## Debt Review — The Legal Lifeline

If you genuinely cannot pay your debts, the National Credit Act gives you a structured way out called **debt review**.

Here is what it does:

- A registered debt counsellor assesses your income, expenses, and debts.
- They negotiate with all your creditors for one consolidated, affordable monthly payment.
- The arrangement is confirmed by a magistrate's court order.
- Creditors **cannot take legal action** against you while you are under review.
- Once you complete the plan, the listing is cleared from your credit record.

Debt review is not bankruptcy. It is not a black mark for life. It is a legal pause button designed to keep working South Africans in their homes and cars while they recover. You can only be removed from debt review by a court or by your counsellor with creditor consent.

## What Debt Collectors Cannot Do

Under the Debt Collectors Act and the NCR's Code of Conduct, a debt collector **cannot**:

- Threaten arrest, criminal prosecution, or violence
- Lie about being a lawyer, sheriff, or police officer
- Contact you at unreasonable hours (before 7am or after 9pm, or on Sundays)
- Discuss your debt with your employer, family, or neighbours
- Add fees or interest the original credit agreement does not allow
- Refuse to give their full name, the creditor's name, and the original amount in writing

If they do any of this, they are breaking the law.

## What to Do RIGHT NOW if a Collector Threatens You

If you are getting threatening calls today, do this:

1. **Stay calm.** They want panic. Do not give it to them.
2. **Do not acknowledge any debt.** Say: "Send me everything in writing."
3. **Record the date, time, name of caller, and exact threats** in a notebook.
4. **Demand a Section 129 notice** if the creditor wants to take action.
5. **Report illegal conduct** to the National Credit Regulator (ncr.org.za) or the Council for Debt Collectors (cfdc.org.za). Both accept written complaints.
6. **Speak to an attorney** if a summons arrives or a garnishee order is threatened.

You have rights. Use them.
