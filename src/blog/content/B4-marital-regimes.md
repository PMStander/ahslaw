# Community of Property vs Accrual — Which Actually Protects You?

Most South Africans cannot tell you which marital regime they are married under. They sign the papers at Home Affairs, walk out as a married couple, and only find out what regime they chose years later — usually in a lawyer's office, usually during a divorce, and usually too late to fix.

That single piece of paper changes everything. It decides whether your spouse's debts become your debts. It decides whether the business you built belongs to you or both of you. It decides what walks out the door if the marriage ends.

This is the plain-language guide we wish every couple read before saying "I do."

## What is a marital regime?

A marital regime is the legal rulebook that governs the money, assets, and debts of a married couple. It tells you and the courts who owns what during the marriage, who is liable for which debts, and how everything gets split if the marriage ends in divorce or death.

In South Africa you have three options. You pick one *before* you marry — or one gets picked for you by default.

## The three options at a glance

| Regime | What it means | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| **In Community of Property** (default if no ANC) | All assets and debts — yours, theirs, before the marriage and during it — merge into one **joint estate**. You own everything 50/50. You owe everything 50/50. | Couples with similar finances, no business risk, simple estates |
| **Out of Community without Accrual** | Total separation. What is yours stays yours. What is theirs stays theirs. No sharing of growth. No sharing of debts. | Second marriages, large pre-existing estates, couples wanting full independence |
| **Out of Community with Accrual** | Each spouse keeps their assets separate during the marriage. On divorce or death, the **growth** (accrual) of both estates is shared equally. | Most modern couples — fairness without entanglement |

Anything other than "in community of property" requires an **antenuptial contract** (ANC) signed by both parties before the wedding and registered with the Deeds Office. No ANC, no choice — the law marries you in community of property automatically.

## The trap of "in community of property"

This is the regime most South Africans default into and the one most South Africans misunderstand.

When you marry in community of property, your separate financial lives end. Two estates become one **joint estate**. Every rand you own, every cent your spouse owns, every car, every property, every retirement annuity — all of it pools together. You each own half.

That sounds romantic until the debts arrive.

In a joint estate you are **jointly and severally liable** for the debts of the marriage. That includes:

- Debts your spouse takes on during the marriage, even if you did not know about them
- The credit card your spouse maxed out before going on holiday with someone else
- The business loan that went bad
- The car finance your spouse signed up for without telling you

If your spouse is sued, your half of the joint estate is on the line. If your spouse is sequestrated (declared insolvent), **the whole joint estate is sequestrated** — including your share. Your house, your savings, your name on the credit bureau. Gone together.

The single biggest myth in South African family law is "in community of property protects me." It does the exact opposite. It binds you to every financial decision your spouse makes — good or bad. This matters especially if your spouse accumulates debt: [in South Africa, civil debt carries serious consequences](/blog/you-cannot-be-arrested-for-debt), and in a joint estate those consequences are shared.

## The "clean break" of accrual

Out of community **with accrual** is the regime most couples actually want, even though most never sign for it.

Here is how it works. On the day you marry, both spouses declare the value of their estates in the antenuptial contract. That is the **starting value**. During the marriage you each own your own assets. You each control your own debts. You each sign for yourselves.

If the marriage ends — through divorce or death — you compare each estate's value at the end against its starting value (adjusted for inflation). The difference is the **accrual** of that estate. The spouse with the smaller accrual is entitled to **half the difference** between the two accruals.

A simple example. You start the marriage with R200,000 in assets. Your spouse starts with R0. Twenty years later you have R3 million; your spouse has R500,000. Your accrual is R2.8 million. Theirs is R500,000. The difference is R2.3 million. Your spouse is entitled to half — R1.15 million.

That is fairness without entanglement. You both share the *growth* of the marriage. You do not share the *risk* of each other's bad financial decisions during it.

Inheritances, donations, and damages for personal injury are excluded from the accrual unless the ANC says otherwise.

## How to find out which regime you're in

If you cannot remember signing an antenuptial contract before your wedding, you are almost certainly married in community of property. The default kicks in the moment you say "I do" without an ANC.

To check formally:

1. Pull out your **marriage certificate** — it will note "ANC" if there is one
2. Search the **Deeds Office** — every registered antenuptial contract is on file
3. Ask the **attorney or notary** who married you (a notary public has to register every ANC)
4. Contact your **Department of Home Affairs** branch — they can confirm the marriage record

If you signed an ANC, get a copy. Read it. Know what you actually agreed to. The contract should specify whether accrual applies and what the starting values were.

## Can you change your regime mid-marriage?

Yes — but it takes a court order.

Section 21 of the **Matrimonial Property Act 88 of 1984** allows married spouses to apply jointly to the High Court to change their matrimonial property system. The court must be satisfied that:

- There are **sound reasons** for the change
- Sufficient **notice** has been given to all creditors
- **No other person will be prejudiced** by the change

The most common reasons are protecting the family from a new business venture's risk, separating estates as wealth grows, or correcting a poorly understood default at the time of marriage.

The process is not cheap and it is not quick. You will need an attorney, you will need to draft a postnuptial contract, and you will need to publish notices. Budget around three to six months and several thousand rand. For most couples sitting in a risky community-of-property marriage, it is still worth every cent.

## What this means for divorce

The **Divorce Act 70 of 1979** gives South Africa one ground for divorce: **irretrievable breakdown** of the marriage. The court cannot refuse a divorce if breakdown is shown.

But how the *estate* gets divided depends entirely on your regime:

- **In community of property** — joint estate is split 50/50, debts and all. The healthy spouse can be left paying off the unhealthy spouse's debt for years.
- **Out of community without accrual** — each spouse walks away with what is in their own name. Often unfair to the spouse who stayed home or earned less.
- **Out of community with accrual** — the growth of both estates is balanced. Usually the fairest outcome.

Custody, contact, and maintenance are governed separately under the **Children's Act 38 of 2005** and apply regardless of marital regime. The "best interests of the child" is the standard the court uses, every time. ## Common misconceptions

- **"In community of property protects me from my spouse's debts."** It does the exact opposite — you become equally liable for them.
- **"Mothers automatically get the children."** Not true. Both parents have equal parental rights. The court decides on the best interests of each specific child.
- **"We agreed everything, so we don't need an attorney."** Even an uncontested divorce needs a court order, and a poorly drafted settlement can hurt both parties for years afterwards.
- **"I'm entitled to half of everything in a divorce."** Only true in community of property. Under accrual you share growth, not assets. Out of community without accrual, you share nothing.
