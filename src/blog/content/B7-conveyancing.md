# Property Transfer Explained: What You Pay, Why It Takes So Long

Your offer was accepted. Congratulations — you signed the deed of sale, the estate agent shook your hand, and you walked out picturing yourself behind the new front door by the weekend.

Then someone said the words "eight to twelve weeks."

If you are buying or selling a home in South Africa for the first or second time, the conveyancing process feels like a black box. Money moves in directions you didn't expect. Attorneys you never asked for suddenly need your ID. The deal seems to stand still for weeks at a time. And nobody quite explains why.

This guide is the explanation. We will walk you through who is involved, what each step actually does, what you pay and what you don't, and why those three months are not wasted time — they are the law protecting your investment.

---

## What Conveyancing Actually Is

**Conveyancing** is the legal process of transferring ownership of immovable property from a seller to a buyer. In South Africa, only a qualified **conveyancing attorney** — admitted by the High Court — can lodge a transfer at the **Deeds Office**. An estate agent cannot do it. A general attorney cannot do it. The bank cannot do it.

Why so strict? Because the title deed is the single document that proves you own your home. If it is wrong, your ownership is wrong. The conveyancer's job is to make sure that when your name appears on that deed, every cent is accounted for, every signature is verified, and every cent owed to council, SARS and the bond-holder is settled.

That care takes time. It is also why conveyancing is regulated by a fixed scale of fees — your attorney is not pricing the work, the law is.

---

## The Four Attorneys Who May Be Involved

Most buyers expect "an attorney." In reality, up to four conveyancers can be touching a single transaction:

- **Transfer attorney** — appointed by the **seller**. Drives the transaction. Drafts the deed of transfer, collects FICA documents from both sides, lodges everything at the Deeds Office.
- **Bond attorney** — appointed by the **buyer's bank**. Registers the new mortgage bond over the property. Without them, the bank will not release the loan.
- **Bond cancellation attorney** — appointed by the **seller's bank**. Cancels the seller's existing bond on the day of registration. Without them, the seller's bond cannot be paid off and the new transfer cannot register.
- **Seller's attorney** (occasionally) — used where the seller wants their own legal review of the agreement, particularly on high-value or estate transfers.

The buyer pays the transfer attorney and the bond attorney. The seller pays the bond cancellation attorney. All three must work in lockstep — if one is slow, everyone waits. This is the single most common reason transfers stall.

## The Timeline — Week by Week

Here is what an average **R1.6 million** transfer with a bond looks like over 8–12 weeks:

**Week 1 — Instruction**
The signed offer to purchase reaches the transfer attorney. They open a file, request FICA documents, and ask for the seller's existing bond account number.

**Week 2 — Bond approval and cancellation figures**
The buyer's bank issues bond approval and instructs the bond attorney. The seller's bank issues bond cancellation figures (the exact rand amount needed to settle the existing bond).

**Weeks 3–4 — Documents and transfer duty**
Both buyer and seller sign transfer documents at the conveyancer's office. The buyer pays the transfer attorney's costs and transfer duty. The conveyancer obtains a **Transfer Duty Receipt** from SARS — the Deeds Office will not register without it.

**Weeks 4–6 — Rates clearance and levy clearance**
The seller pays municipal rates 4–6 months in advance. The municipality issues a **rates clearance certificate**. If the property is in a complex or estate, the body corporate or HOA issues a **levy clearance certificate**. These two certificates are the most common cause of delay.

**Weeks 6–8 — Lodgement**
All three sets of documents (transfer, new bond, bond cancellation) are lodged at the Deeds Office on the same day. They must be lodged together — none can register without the others.

**Weeks 8–10 — Examination and registration**
Deeds Office examiners review every document. Errors send the file back. A clean file registers in 7–10 working days. On registration, the property is legally yours.

**Day of registration**
The bank releases your loan. The seller is paid. Keys change hands. You are home.

## Costs at a Glance

| Item | Paid By | Approximate Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer duty | Buyer | 0% on first R1.1m; graduated above |
| Conveyancing fees (transfer) | Buyer | Scale fee on property value |
| Bond registration costs | Buyer | Scale fee on bond value |
| Deeds Office fees | Buyer | R1,200–R3,000 |
| FICA / admin / Postage & Petties | Both | R1,500–R3,000 |
| Bond cancellation fees | Seller | R4,000–R6,000 |
| Rates clearance | Seller | 4–6 months rates in advance |
| Levy clearance (if applicable) | Seller | 2–3 months levies in advance |
| Compliance certificates (electrical, beetle, gas, plumbing) | Seller | R2,000–R10,000 |

The buyer's biggest single line is usually **transfer duty**. That deserves its own table.

## Transfer Duty — 2026 Brackets

Transfer duty is a tax paid to SARS on every property purchase, separate from the price you pay the seller. The 2026 brackets are:

| Property Value | Transfer Duty |
|---|---|
| R0 – R1,100,000 | **0%** |
| R1,100,001 – R1,512,500 | **3%** on amount above R1,100,000 |
| R1,512,501 – R2,117,500 | R12,375 + **6%** on amount above R1,512,500 |
| R2,117,501 – R2,722,500 | R48,675 + **8%** on amount above R2,117,500 |
| R2,722,501 – R12,100,000 | R97,075 + **11%** on amount above R2,722,500 |
| Above R12,100,000 | R1,128,600 + **13%** on amount above R12,100,000 |

A buyer purchasing at R1.6 million pays roughly R17,625 in transfer duty. A buyer at R3 million pays R127,550. First-time buyers under R1.1 million pay nothing — which is why that band matters so much.

Transfer duty must be paid before lodgement, not after. SARS does not negotiate.

## FICA — Why It Adds Delays

The **Financial Intelligence Centre Act (FICA)** requires conveyancers to verify the identity, address and source of funds of every party. It is not optional. It is not a formality. The Deeds Office will reject a transfer where FICA is incomplete.

What you actually need to provide:

- A clear, certified copy of your **ID or passport**
- A **proof of residence** not older than **3 months** (utility bill, bank statement, retail account, lease)
- Your **SARS tax number**
- For married buyers: a copy of your **marriage certificate** and, where applicable, your **antenuptial contract**
- For companies and trusts: registration documents, resolutions and IDs of all directors/trustees

Three things trip people up: an ID that has expired, a utility bill older than three months, and a marital regime that does not match what the buyer thinks it is. A buyer who believes they are married out of community of property but signed without an antenuptial contract is in fact married in community — and the spouse must co-sign the transfer. [Read our guide to the three marital regimes](/blog/community-of-property-vs-accrual) before you get to the conveyancer's table.

## Common Causes of Delay

Most "stuck" transfers fail on one of these:

1. **Outstanding municipal rates.** If the seller is behind, the municipality will not issue a rates clearance certificate until everything is paid plus 4–6 months in advance.
2. **Expired ID or stale proof of address.** FICA reset, file paused.
3. **Marital regime mismatches.** A buyer who believes they are married out of community of property but signed without an antenuptial contract is in fact married in community of property — and the spouse must sign too.
4. **Bond approval delays.** The bank wants more financials. The whole file waits.
5. **Property description errors** in the deed of sale — wrong erf number, wrong title deed reference.
6. **Levy clearance** held up by a body corporate that cannot find its records.

The single best thing a buyer or seller can do is respond to their conveyancer the same day. Most "three-month transfers" are really three-week transfers with two months of waiting for someone to send a document.

## Foreigners Buying Property in South Africa

Before you buy, also consider whether anyone is currently occupying the property. Even without a signed lease, occupants in South Africa have constitutional protection under the PIE Act — [a landlord or new owner cannot remove them without a court order](/blog/pie-act-locks).

South Africa imposes **no general restrictions** on foreign property ownership. A foreign buyer pays the same transfer duty and follows the same conveyancing process as a South African buyer.

What is different:

- **Exchange control** rules apply when the foreign buyer eventually sells and wants to repatriate proceeds. The original purchase funds should be brought in through an authorised dealer (a South African bank) so a Reserve Bank-approved trail exists.
- **Bond finance is restricted.** Foreign buyers who are not resident in SA typically qualify for a bond of only **50% of the purchase price**. The other 50% must come from offshore.
- A South African **tax number** is required for transfer.

Permanent residents and work-visa holders are treated more like locals.

## Buyer vs Seller — Who Pays What

| Cost | Buyer | Seller |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer duty | Yes | No |
| Conveyancing fees (transfer) | Yes | No |
| Bond registration | Yes | No |
| Deeds Office fees | Yes | No |
| Bond cancellation | No | Yes |
| Rates clearance | No | Yes |
| Levy clearance | No | Yes |
| Compliance certificates | No | Yes |
| Estate agent's commission | No | Yes |

A buyer should budget roughly **8–10% of the purchase price** above the price itself. A seller's costs come out of the proceeds at registration — they almost never write a cheque on the day.
