When someone is charged with a very serious offence in South Africa, bail is no longer the normal starting point. For offences listed in Schedule 6 of the Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977, the law flips the usual approach. The court must keep the accused in custody unless that person can show something specific: exceptional circumstances that, in the interests of justice, allow release.

This is one of the strictest bail rules in our law. Families and accused persons often hear the phrase “exceptional circumstances” and wonder what it actually requires. Here is a clear explanation.

### The legal starting point

Section 60(11)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Act says that where an accused is charged with a Schedule 6 offence, the court shall order that the person remain in custody until the case is finalised — unless the accused, having been given a reasonable opportunity, produces evidence that satisfies the court that exceptional circumstances exist which in the interests of justice permit release.

This creates a reverse onus. In ordinary bail applications the State usually has to show why the person should stay locked up. In Schedule 6 matters the accused must prove, on a balance of probabilities, that there are exceptional circumstances justifying release.

The Constitutional Court confirmed that this approach is constitutional in S v Dlamini; S v Dladla and Others; S v Joubert; S v Schietekat 1999 (4) SA 623 (CC). The Court made it clear that the requirement is demanding, but it is not impossible to meet.

### What counts as a Schedule 6 offence?

Schedule 6 covers some of the most serious crimes. These include (among others):

*   Murder in certain circumstances (for example where it was planned or premeditated)
*   Rape in specified circumstances
*   Robbery with aggravating circumstances (often involving a firearm or other dangerous weapon)
*   Certain forms of human trafficking
*   Some drug-related offences involving large quantities

The exact list is set out in the Act. If the charge sheet refers to Schedule 6, the stricter test applies.

### What “exceptional circumstances” actually means

The Act does not define the phrase. Courts have said it means something unusual, extraordinary, remarkable, peculiar or different from the ordinary run of cases.

Importantly, the Constitutional Court held that the circumstances do not have to be completely different in kind from the normal factors listed in section 60(4) to (9) of the Act (flight risk, interference with witnesses, public safety, strength of the State’s case, and so on). Ordinary factors can become exceptional if they are established to an exceptional degree, or if their combined weight is so compelling that release is justified despite the seriousness of the charge.

What does not usually qualify on its own:

*   Having a fixed address
*   Being employed
*   Having a family to support
*   Being a first offender
*   Needing to look after children or elderly parents

These are ordinary circumstances. Courts repeatedly say that “run-of-the-mill” personal factors are not enough for Schedule 6 bail.

What can help tip the scale (when properly proved):

*   A genuinely weak State case or a strong, verifiable alibi
*   Serious medical conditions that cannot be properly managed in custody
*   Clear evidence that the accused poses no real risk of absconding, interfering with witnesses or re-offending, combined with other compelling factors
*   A combination of factors that, taken together, make continued detention unjust

Each case is decided on its own facts. There is no closed list.

### The practical reality

Schedule 6 bail applications are difficult. The starting point is detention. The accused must put proper evidence before the court — usually through affidavits, supporting documents, and sometimes oral evidence. Vague statements or untested claims rarely succeed.

Courts also still consider the ordinary interests-of-justice factors. Even if exceptional circumstances are shown, the court must be satisfied that release is in the interests of justice. Conditions can be imposed (reporting to the police, surrender of passport, no contact with certain people, and so on), but the threshold remains high.

### Why early legal advice matters

Timing is critical. A poorly prepared first bail application can make a later one harder. The quality of the evidence, the way the personal circumstances are presented, and the ability to address the specific risks the State raises all make a difference.

Families often feel powerless when a loved one is facing a Schedule 6 charge. Understanding that the law requires more than ordinary personal circumstances is the first step. The second is ensuring that any application is properly prepared with real, verifiable evidence.
